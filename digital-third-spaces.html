An essay with a scorecard

Digital third spaces

Home is the first place, work the second. The third is where you go to be among people for no reason at all — and more and more of it lives on a screen.

In 1989 the sociologist Ray Oldenburg published The Great Good Place, an argument that the coffee shops, pubs, barbershops and general stores of ordinary life were not incidental. They were infrastructure: the places where neighbours became acquaintances, where strangers argued about nothing, where a town learned what it thought. He called them third places, and he worried, loudly, that suburban America was paving them over.

Thirty-odd years later, a lot of people answer his question — where do you go to just hang out? — with the name of a Discord server, a guild, a subreddit or a group chat. Oldenburg himself was skeptical that anything on a screen could count. This page takes his own checklist and runs the internet through it.

Oldenburg’s eight traits

His test for a third place, paraphrased. A place doesn’t need all eight, but the good ones have most.

    Rooms, not feeds

    Most of the argument fits in one distinction.

    Oldenburg never had to spell out that a café has walls, because every café does. Online, walls are optional, and the biggest platforms removed them on purpose. That split — between places that behave like rooms and places that behave like feeds — predicts the scorecard below better than any other single factor.

    A room

    • Has edges. You can tell who is in it, roughly how many, and when you have left.
    • Has memory. The same people are there tomorrow, and they remember yesterday.
    • Has presence. You can see who is around right now and wander over.
    • Is shaped by its regulars, who set the tone and greet the newcomer.

    A feed

    • Has no edges. Your audience is whoever the ranking decides, at any size.
    • Has no memory. Each scroll is a fresh draw; you rarely see the same stranger twice.
    • Has no presence. Everyone is a post, not a person who is “here.”
    • Is shaped by a ranking that optimises for attention, not for regulars.

    You can be lonely in a feed of millions and at home in a room of twelve. Regulars are impossible without memory, conversation is impossible without edges, and “a home away from home” is impossible in a place that rearranges itself every time you open it.

    Two things stand out. The top of the table is dominated by small, bounded, persistent spaces, the ones that look most like rooms. And the group chat scores well while failing the two traits that matter most to Oldenburg: it is not neutral ground and it levels nothing, because everyone in it already knows each other. It is a wonderful thing, but it is the living room extended, not a third place. The whole point of his third place was meeting people outside your circle.

    Pick a server, forum, game or chat you actually spend time in and answer honestly.

    What a good one needs

    If you are building or running one, the traits turn into design choices.

    1. Keep it small enough to have regulars. Conversation stops scaling long before servers do. When a space outgrows the point where people recognise each other, split it into rooms rather than letting it become a stage.
    2. Show who is here. The single most café-like feature online is a presence list: names that light up, a voice channel with three people idling in it. It is the digital version of seeing friends through the window.
    3. Make dropping in free. No scheduling, no performance, no obligation to post. Lurking is how most people first walk into a bar, too.
    4. Prefer persistent names to follower counts. A stable handle lets people build a reputation inside the room; a visible metric imports status from outside it and breaks the leveler.
    5. Give it a host, not an algorithm. Oldenburg’s places had a proprietor who knew the regulars. Online, that is a moderator with a name, a face and judgment — not a ranking system tuned for time-on-site.
    6. Let the activity be an excuse. The game, the hobby or the show gives people a reason to show up. The good spaces let conversation take over from it.

    The catch: who owns the room

    A café owner makes money selling coffee, so a regular who nurses one cup for three hours is tolerated, even loved. Most digital gathering places are rented from a platform that makes money from attention, and that difference leaks into everything. Ranked feeds replace conversation with performance because performance holds attention. Badges and counters arrive because status is engaging. And the room can disappear on a business decision: Club Penguin and AOL Instant Messenger both shut down in 2017, taking their regulars’ shared histories with them.

    A third place you rent is only as permanent as the landlord’s quarterly plan.

    That doesn’t make digital third places fake. Researchers studying online games in the mid-2000s found that guilds did real third-place work, especially in mixing people who would never otherwise meet. It does mean the ones that last tend to be the ones whose community has some stake in the walls: volunteer-run forums, self-hosted servers, guilds that outlive the game they started in.

    A short history

    1. 1985The WELL opens, a dial-up conferencing system in the San Francisco Bay Area and one of the first online communities with a strong sense of place.
    2. 1989Oldenburg publishes The Great Good Place.
    3. 1990LambdaMOO, a text-based world built of rooms people could walk between, goes online.
    4. 1993Howard Rheingold’s The Virtual Community, largely about the WELL, argues that online groups can be real communities.
    5. 2000Robert Putnam’s Bowling Alone documents the decline of American civic and social life.
    6. 2003Second Life launches; virtual worlds become places with addresses.
    7. 2004World of Warcraft launches, and its guilds become one of the largest third-place experiments ever run.
    8. 2006Steinkuehler and Williams publish “Where Everybody Knows Your Name,” finding that online games meet most of Oldenburg’s criteria.
    9. 2015Discord launches for gamers and becomes the default clubhouse for hobbies of every kind.
    10. 2020Pandemic lockdowns close physical third places almost everywhere at once; game worlds and voice channels absorb the overflow.
    11. 2023The U.S. Surgeon General issues an advisory on the “epidemic of loneliness and isolation,” naming the loss of shared social spaces as part of the problem.
