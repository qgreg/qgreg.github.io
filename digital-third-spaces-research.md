# Digital third spaces: research notes

Working notes behind the page. These cover two hypotheses: (1) remote work
removed the commute that used to separate home from work, and (2) audiences,
like workers, are less willing to travel. Each finding is marked by how well it
is supported. "Checked" means it was confirmed in a primary or close-to-primary
source during research. "Secondary" means it was found only in press coverage.

## Before the pandemic: the boundary between home and work

- **Boundary theory.** Ashforth, Kreiner & Fugate, "All in a day's work:
  Boundaries and micro role transitions," *Academy of Management Review* 25(3),
  472–491 (2000). It treats each move between roles as crossing a boundary. Roles
  sit on a continuum from *segmented* (separate times and places, large
  transitions eased by rites of passage) to *integrated* (blurred, small
  transitions, but boundaries that are hard to keep). Checked (abstract and
  secondary citations).
- **Segmentation and integration as personal styles.** Christena Nippert-Eng,
  *Home and Work* (1996). It describes "segmentors" and "integrators" and the
  physical props people use to mark the boundary, such as keys, calendars and
  clothes. Secondary (cited through later papers).
- **The commute as a transition.** Jachimowicz, Lee Cunningham, Staats, Gino &
  Menges, "Between Home and Work: Commuting as an Opportunity for Role
  Transitions," *Organization Science* 32(1), 64–85 (2021). The study used three
  field studies (n = 1,736), including a four-week intervention. The commute
  serves as a liminal transition between roles. Thinking ahead to the work role
  during the commute ("role-clarifying prospection") reduced the harm of long
  commutes. Most of the data predate the pandemic, and the authors flag that
  remote workers lose this transition. Checked.

**For the page:** the field already had words for what changed. The commute
was a *rite of passage* between segmented roles. Remote work moved most
workers towards integration without giving them a new rite. In the page's
terms, the boundary used to be geographic. Now it has to be built from medium,
configuration and mise en scène, for example closing the work laptop and
opening a personal machine.

## Now: remote work in 2025–26

- **Scale.** Working from home accounts for about a quarter of paid workdays
  among Americans aged 20–64 as of 2025. Desired rates exceed actual rates in
  every major demographic group. Source: Buckman, Barrero, Bloom & Davis,
  "Measuring Work from Home" (NBER working paper, Feb 2025). Checked.
- **Arrangements.** By spring 2025, 13% of full-time employees were fully
  remote, 27% were hybrid and 60% were fully on site (Survey of Working
  Arrangements and Attitudes, WFH Research). These are shares of workers, not of
  days. Checked. Monthly updates continue, so the latest wave should be checked
  before citing.
- **The boundary has eroded.** Microsoft's 2025 Work Trend Index describes an
  "infinite workday." Reported figures include meetings after 8 p.m. up about
  16% year over year and about 29% of active workers back in their inboxes by
  10 p.m. Secondary: outlets disagree on some numbers, so verify against
  Microsoft's report before citing.

## Audiences: are they less willing to travel?

- **Broadway's commuter audience is shrinking.** The Broadway League's
  demographics reports put suburban residents (Westchester, Rockland, Long
  Island and northern New Jersey) at 15.5% of admissions in 2018–19, 13.6% in
  2022–23, about 13% in 2023–24, and under 13% in 2024–25, the lowest in 30
  years. The share was once about 20%. Meanwhile total attendance has recovered
  to 14.66M in 2024–25, against 14.8M in 2018–19, carried by tourists and New
  York City residents. Checked (League figures via trade press).
  - **Interpretation:** the Hollywood Reporter links the suburban decline to
    pandemic habit changes and remote work. That's reporting, not a survey
    finding, but it's the closest match to the hypothesis: the audience that
    used to combine a show with a commute into the city has thinned, while
    people who travel anyway (tourists) have not.
- **National attendance is down.** The NEA Survey of Public Participation in
  the Arts (2022, covering July 2021–July 2022) found attendance rates declined
  from 2017 for almost every type of performance, including musicals and
  plays. Over 2002–2022, live attendance fell by 2–8 points depending on type.
  Checked. Caveat: the survey period was still heavily affected by the
  pandemic.
- **The reasons are mostly money.** WolfBrown's Audience Outlook Monitor, using
  Australian data, found cost overtook the virus as the main barrier by October
  2022, with 44% attending less often. In 2023, 41% named cost as the top
  barrier. Briefings also report more people watching entertainment at home.
  Checked for Australia, and suggestive only for the US.
- **Regional theatre recovery is partial.** In Greater Philadelphia, 71% of
  theatres saw more people than the previous year, but only 41% are back to
  pre-pandemic levels, and about 30% of audiences use public transit (Greater
  Philadelphia Cultural Alliance, 2025). Checked (press report).
- **Broadcast complements live performance.** National Theatre Live: on
  28M+ tickets at 54 English venues (2009–13), simulcasts did not cannibalise
  live attendance and raised local attendance about 6.4% in London (Bakhshi &
  Throsby, Nesta working paper, 2014). Checked. Film festivals report a similar
  pattern: virtual programming works as a complement, especially for dispersed
  audiences (Eventive, 2025). Secondary.

**For the page:** the hypothesis holds in a narrower, more precise form.
Audiences are not refusing to go out. Tourists and city residents keep Broadway
full. What thinned is the commuter audience, people for whom the show was
attached to a trip into the city they no longer make. That's the same boundary
that disappeared for workers. The opportunity for creators isn't replacing
live performance. It's reaching the audience the commute used to deliver. The
NT Live evidence suggests that work can grow the live audience rather than
compete with it.

## Other facts confirmed

- **Mozilla Hubs.** Mozilla announced the shutdown on 13–15 February 2024 and
  turned off the service on 31 May 2024. The code continues as Hubs Community
  Edition, maintained by the Hubs Foundation. Checked.
