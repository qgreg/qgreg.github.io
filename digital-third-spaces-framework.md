# Digital third spaces: working framework

The shape of the argument so far, so it survives the conversation. The
evidence is in `digital-third-spaces-research.md`. Claims marked
*hypothesis* are not yet findings.

## 1. Premise: one reality

Online experience is part of one reality, not a second world. "Digital" names
a medium, not a place you go to. Researchers call the opposite habit *digital
dualism* (Jurgenson, 2011). The useful question about any setting is what
people experience there, never whether it is "real."

## 2. Vocabulary: five layers

| Layer | Question | Theatre analogue |
|---|---|---|
| Medium | What kind of thing is it, and what does it allow? | Stage play vs film vs radio drama |
| Mise en scène | What is arranged to be perceived? | Set, light, costume, where the actors stand |
| Configuration | What parameters are set, and by whom? (*Settings* are its values.) | Running time, house rules, who is allowed backstage |
| Conditions | What varies from one occasion to the next without anyone choosing? | Friday house vs Sunday house |
| Experience | What do people go through? | The performance as it was actually received |

- **Test:** if it can change without switching the medium, it is configuration.
  If nobody chose it, it is a condition.
- **Ownership:** who owns the configuration matters as much as what it is. A
  third place is a setting whose configuration is not set by anyone with
  authority over you, which is Oldenburg's "neutral ground."
- **Regulars are a condition, not a configuration.** You can configure *for*
  them, but you cannot configure them.

## 3. Central example: same chair, different place

A remote worker joins a Teams stand-up on a company laptop, then later a Hubs
room on a personal PC. The body, chair, room and network are the same. Every
layer above them changed. The device is environment, and the employer's
management policy on it is configuration.

## 4. Third places, read through the layers

- **Oldenburg's eight traits** sort roughly into environment (accessible,
  low profile), configuration (neutral ground, regulars, conversation) and
  experience (leveler, playful mood, home away from home).
- **Rooms vs feeds** is a difference of configuration: edges, memory,
  encounter.
- **The scorecard** measures whether a configured environment reliably
  produces the experiences.
- **Spatial computing** enriches the medium and mise en scène, involving more
  senses and more of the body, but still depends on configuration. WebXR
  lowers the access barrier.

## 5. Shape of the present: physical language

- **Decoupling** (what changed): location and role used to be coupled, and
  the commute was the coupling. Now they are independent variables.
- **Hysteresis** (where we are): remote work rose, fell back partly, and
  levelled off well above baseline. *Verify with the WFH time series.*
- **Activation energy** (why it stays): the pandemic paid the switching costs
  all at once. The WFH researchers' "forced experiment."
- **Annealing vs quenching** (how to respond): slow settling vs abrupt
  mandates that freeze defects in place. *Editorial frame, not evidence.*
- **Keep the shapes distinct:** hysteresis (WFH), accelerated trend (suburban
  Broadway share, already falling before 2020), relaxation (total Broadway
  attendance, back near baseline).

## 6. Economics: Baumol and AI

- **Baumol's cost disease** (Baumol & Bowen, *Performing Arts: The Economic
  Dilemma*, 1966). Live performance cannot raise output per hour of labour,
  so it grows relatively more expensive. This is consistent with cost being
  audiences' top barrier.
- **The cure that keeps it live:** enlarge the house, not the cast. NT Live
  evidence suggests broadcast adds to live attendance. Live performance in a
  shared space scales the audience *and* keeps the conditions. A recording
  keeps only the mise en scène.
- **AI intensifies Baumol** (*hypothesis*). As codifiable work gets more
  productive, human encounter becomes the relatively scarce thing. Verify
  Aghion, Jones & Jones (2017) on Baumol effects and AI growth.
- **An AI companion fails Oldenburg's core test.** There is no one else
  there to meet.
- **The value of an hour depends on its configuration.** The hour itself is
  fixed. Its leverage is set by the configuration it sits in, and whoever owns
  the configuration decides who captures the gain.

## 6a. Socialisation and creativity

- **Organisational socialisation** is an established field (Van Maanen &
  Schein, 1979). Much of it used to happen through physical co-location.
- **Remote work siloed collaboration.** Firm-wide remote work at Microsoft
  (about 61,000 employees) made collaboration networks more static and siloed,
  with fewer ties across groups (Yang et al., *Nature Human Behaviour*, 2022).
  *Verify details.*
- **New ideas travel through weak ties** (Granovetter, 1973), which are the
  ties third places produce and the commute used to supply.
- **Socialisation is a Baumol activity.** Mentoring, onboarding and corridor
  talk cannot be made more productive per hour. As AI cheapens codifiable work,
  encounter grows relatively more expensive, and firms are pushed to cut it
  just as it matters most for creativity.
- **Same cure as the theatre:** enlarge the house, not the cast. Configure
  encounter so an hour of it reaches more people and more weak ties.

## 6b. Baumol's blessings

- **Some things don't compress, and that is their value.** In care and in
  time spent together, the hours are not a cost of producing the good; they
  *are* the good. A compressed bedside visit, mentoring session or
  performance is a lesser one.
- **Baumol's own optimistic point** (*The Cost Disease*, 2012): productivity
  growth elsewhere means society can afford the rising relative cost.
- **The blessing framing goes further:** if AI agents compress codifiable
  work, the dividend can be spent on what doesn't compress.
- **Design rule: share encounter, don't compress it.** "Enlarge the house"
  shares a performance with more people without shortening it.
- **The phrase:** one search found no prior use of "Baumol's blessing."
  That's not proof of originality, so check again before claiming it.

## 7. Subject statement

Baumol says human encounter keeps getting relatively more expensive, and AI
agents accelerate that just as encounter becomes what matters most. With place
no longer arranging it, the work is to configure encounter, for employees and
for audiences, so it scales without losing what makes it live.

Alternative, from the blessing side: Caring for people and time together
don't compress. That is Baumol's blessing, not only his disease. As AI agents
compress the work that can be compressed, the question is whether the time
they free is spent on encounter, and how to configure it now that place no
longer does.

Possible section title: "After the commute: configuring encounter in the age
of AI agents."

## 7a. Thesis (*hypothesis*)

The pandemic decoupled where people are from what they are doing. Baumol says
being among people will keep getting relatively more expensive, and AI
accelerates that. Whoever learns to configure encounter without relying on
the commute owns what becomes scarce.

## 11. Numbers in human connection

- **Hubs room cap: 25 by default,** adjustable by the room owner, with
  overflow watching from the lobby. It's a *medium* limit, set by device
  performance on phones and standalone headsets, not a social one. Checked
  (Mozilla docs; a mirrored copy gives 24, raisable to 30).
- **Conversation splits at about four speakers.** Freely forming
  conversation groups rarely sustain more than about four people before
  splitting (Dunbar, Duncan & Nettle, 1995). *Verify.* With spatial audio,
  25 people become several small conversations. On a video call, 25 people
  are one conversation with about 24 listeners.
- **Team size:** the "two-pizza team" is Amazon's rule (Bezos), not Scrum's.
  The 2020 Scrum Guide says a team is "typically 10 or fewer people."
- **Communication pairs grow as n(n−1)/2:** 10 people make 45 pairs, 25
  make 300, and 150 make 11,175.
- **Dunbar's layers** run about 5, 15, 50 and 150, each roughly three times
  the last. The 150 figure is contested (Lindenfors et al., 2021). *Verify.*
- **No single natural number;** a nested series of them. The design question
  is which layer a configuration serves.
- **Classrooms:** no single studied optimum.
  - **Tennessee STAR** (1985–89, randomised): small classes of 13–17 against
    regular classes of 22–25. Small classes helped, most in early grades and
    for disadvantaged students.
  - **Other studies are mixed:** Glass & Smith (1978) found benefits growing
    below about 15–20. Hoxby (2000) found no effect from natural variation.
  - **Bloom's "2 sigma problem" (1984):** one-to-one tutoring outperformed
    conventional classes by about two standard deviations. Later
    meta-analyses find smaller but real tutoring effects. *Verify.*
  - **Relevance:** tutoring is a pure Baumol case, and AI tutoring is pitched
    as the cure. Whether care in teaching compresses is the
    Baumol's-blessings question in its sharpest form.
- **Dunbar as a frame:**
  - **The layers are a time budget.** Each layer is kept up by time spent on
    it, with the most time going to the innermost few. *Verify the
    proportions.* Relationships are a Baumol good: their maintenance doesn't
    compress.
  - **Online networks appear to obey the same limits** (Dunbar, 2016, on
    social media networks). *Verify.* More connections isn't more
    relationships.
  - **Decoupling thinned the outer layers.** The 50–150 layer of
    acquaintances and weak ties was the one place used to supply; the
    Microsoft network study shows cross-group ties thinning. Inner layers
    survive online.
  - **Third places serve the outer layers.** That's why their loss matters
    for creativity, and why configuring encounter is the work.
