Free-fall / microgravity

Newton's Third Law Ball

In free fall there is no floor to push against, so every move costs momentum you cannot get back. Push off a wall and you are committed to a straight line until you hit something.

The whole sport becomes budgeting: each player gets a handful of pushes per point, and the throw that scores is also the throw that sends you drifting the wrong way.

Lunar surface · 0.17 g

The Long Court

A basketball court sized for Earth legs is a joke at one-sixth gravity — a standing jump rises nearly five metres and hangs for five full seconds.

So you stretch the court six-fold and raise the rim, and the game stops being about speed. It becomes chess with flight times: you commit to an arc, and everyone has several seconds to watch you be wrong.

Rotating habitat · spin gravity

Coriolis Handball

Spin gravity is a lie the floor tells your inner ear. Nothing thrown across the room travels straight: it curves sideways, and the direction of the curve flips depending on which way you throw.

Home teams learn the drift the way sailors learn a tide. Visitors throw straight at the goal and watch the ball bend politely around it.

The fine print

  • Every number comes from the same elite-human launch conditions — take-off speed and angle — run through projectile motion at that world's surface gravity. Only g changes between venues.
  • The math is vacuum ballistics: point mass, no air. That is nearly exact on the Moon and honest enough on Mars. It flatters Earth badly — a real 49 m/s home run travels about 120 m, not the 200 m a vacuum promises, because the atmosphere eats the difference. Earth's row is best read as "if someone pumped the air out of the stadium."
  • Jumping and throwing scale cleanly with gravity. Running does not. Sprinting needs the traction and downforce that gravity supplies, so below roughly half a g athletes bounce instead of stride, and the 100 m dash gets slower, not faster. No sprint numbers here for exactly that reason.
  • Bodies are left out of it too. Months at low gravity thin bone and shrink the heart, so the first lunar record-holders would likely be residents, not visiting Earth athletes.
  • Everything is calibrated so the Earth column lands on today's actual world records. Trust the ratios; hold the decimals loosely.
